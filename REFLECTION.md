# Reflection

One significant takeaway was that understanding how the sensing algorithms and tracking pipelines work helped us design a more reliable interaction. For example, Aurora’s original grabbing logic required continuous fist detection, which caused unintended drops when hand tracking was briefly lost. She revised it so that, once an object was grabbed, it remained held until an open palm was explicitly detected. This distinguished missing tracking data from an intentional release. Similarly, Yuwen improved blink detection by defining an appropriate range of eye-openness values, producing a more consistent signal. Chunzi encountered difficulties with unstable pose detection, as the original crossed-arm gesture did not reliably match her seated movements. By recording and analysing MediaPipe videos, she simplified the drinking trigger to raising both hands above their resting positions for two seconds. She also resolved conflicts between walking and drinking by prioritising the gestures and locking movement and leg animations during drinking. These iterations taught us that reliability depends not only on recognising an action, but also on interpreting uncertainty and translating detection results into meaningful behaviour.

A remaining limitation was the complexity of controlling walking and turning through one person’s upper-body movements. Combining these controls made the elephant difficult to steer precisely and increased the performer’s coordination demands. In a future iteration, we would explore assigning walking and turning to two separate performers, allowing each person to focus on a simpler action. However, this could also increase the need for communication between participants. We would compare both arrangements through user testing to understand which offers the best balance of precision, learnability and collaborative enjoyment.
